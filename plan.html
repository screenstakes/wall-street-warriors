Wharton's guide, word for word, lists what you are not evaluated on: your ranking, how many trades you make, whether you beat other teams, whether you make money. Fewer trades costs you nothing.

Two or three companies each. The whole Treasury ladder is one idea, not ten — one thesis, one paragraph, one person defending it.

Spread the stock sleeve across eight to ten different areas, one name in each. Spreading across areas does more for you than piling up names inside one.

The closest real-world comparison: the ANU Student Managed Fund runs A$897,000 with about five students on stock selection, and caps itself at 12 active positions. Their stated reason is that 7–12 names “balances manageability and diversification.” Same team size, real money, and they landed on twelve.
Why more names buys almost nothing

Each extra name buys less than the last.

Going from 20 names to 50 buys 0.9 of a percentage point of annual risk. From 50 to 100, 0.3. You pay for that with thirty and then fifty more companies somebody has to be able to defend.

Be honest about this in the IPS. The research does not agree on a number — published answers run from 8 to over 300, and they differ because of what you measure, not what is true. Measure swing and the curve flattens fast. Measure the chance of falling short over twenty years and it does not. Laura needs the second one. So do not claim the maths says twelve. Say it is a judgement, and say what it rests on.

My own calculation: a 40% single-stock swing, 0.25 average correlation. State both assumptions and it holds up.

The number that settles it

The index fund does the diversifying.

Three hundredths of a percentage point apart. An S&P 500 fund is roughly fifty companies in a single decision.

This is the sentence that makes it judge-proof: the ETF core is what diversifies Laura. The eight to twelve stocks are not the diversification engine and must never be described as one — they are your active bets, sized so that being wrong on every single one of them still leaves her funded.

Framed that way, all the research arguing you need forty or a hundred names stops applying. Those studies are about portfolios made only of individual stocks. There is no spread around the market when you own the market.

You cannot buy the whole ladder. It costs more than the entire account, and you would own no shares at all. So “a high degree of certainty” cannot mean fully matched — it has to mean a funded near-term ladder plus a growth sleeve with a stated confidence level. That is a better answer anyway, and almost no team will say it.

WInS is where you show the technique. Two rungs demonstrate cash-flow matching exactly as well as ten.

The process, cut down

Two habits. That is the whole system.

1 · No note, no Confirm

Four lines into the WInS box before you place any trade. About forty seconds, inside something you are already doing.

2 · Sixty seconds each on Sunday

Round robin on the call. One number you watched, one thing you'd do differently, one line on how we decided. Federico types it.

Why only two. A habit takes about 66 days of daily repetition to form. Your season is 68 days and a trade happens maybe fifteen times. Nothing here will ever become automatic — so the only thing that works is hanging the record on a moment that already exists. The WInS note box already sits between Review Order and Confirm. You built a ceremony around a gate that was already there.
Gawande's surgical checklist cut deaths nearly in half as an enforced pause in the room, and produced no significant improvement across 101 hospitals when it was mandated as paperwork. Same checklist. One hospital measured 100% documented compliance against 3.5% actual. Paperwork outlives the behaviour.
What gets cut

Keep two, downgrade four, delete one.

Bear case → spoken

Said at the meeting, one line in the minutes. Writing a case against a friend's idea is the highest social cost in the whole process, and it is documented as the first thing to go quiet.

Fit check → folded in

Lines three and four of the note already say who it is for. Asking twice is asking twice.

Valuation → new stocks only

Joe runs one for a new single-stock position. Not for adding to something you hold, not for a fund.

Poll → thumbs

Reactions on the card's thread, or a show of hands on Sunday. Do not build a voting system.

Delete Federico's same-day log row. It duplicates the Excel export WInS already produces. One person exports and reconciles every Friday — ten minutes a week instead of a daily obligation. The only thing lost is the vote count, and the minutes already hold that.

Anyone can say “pull” and move any trade up to the full process, with no reason required. Change the current rule that a HOLD needs a reason — making someone justify an objection is how you stop objections. Even trained professionals using a proper triage tool mis-sort about a third of the time, so correcting a mis-sort has to be free.

Watch for the known trap: whatever tier lets you skip the bear case is the tier every trade will mysteriously qualify for.

Before Friday

Break things while it is still free.

Practice is wiped Friday Sep 25 at 3pm your time, during school. So the real deadline is Thursday evening. Nothing you do here costs anything.

  • Session Rules page — in WInS, Portfolio Simulation → Portfolio Summary → Session Rules. Screenshot it. It shows the position limit, commissions, starting cash and trading dates. Do this one first.
  • Try to buy 30% of the account in one ETF. The default position limit is 25%. If VOO at 30% gets rejected, you need four days to redesign, not four minutes on Sunday.
  • Try to buy SGOV. If the platform rejects it, that settles SGOV versus BIL with evidence instead of argument.
  • Find the bonds page. Do Treasuries maturing late 2032 and late 2033 actually exist? Buy one and watch when it fills.
  • Paste a 120-word note. Does it cut off? Where?
  • Do bond orders even have a note box? If not, you have to add the note afterwards, and that is a different flow to learn now.
  • Export Transaction History to Excel. Does the file contain the note text? October needs the note exactly as it appears in WInS.
  • Place an order at night. Confirm it sits pending and fills at the next open. This single test is what makes the Sunday-night plan safe.
  • Two people logged in at once. Does it break? You share one login.

And then

Most weeks, nothing happens.

Between Sep 29 and Nov 6 expect six to twelve more trades in total. Most weeks will contain zero. That is the strategy working, not the team being idle.

What justifies a trade: a rebalancing rule you wrote down firing, news that actually breaks a thesis, deploying Pile C on a trigger you declared in advance, or one glide-path step — a third Treasury rung in October, which is the single most valuable discretionary trade of your season because it makes “de-risk by 2031” visible in the portfolio instead of just on paper.
What to resist: selling because something is down. Trading so you have something to write about. Reacting to a headline you had no rule for. Tidying the portfolio in the last week — the Nov 6 snapshot is what the final report analyses, and a pointless week-six trade is visible.
Every Sunday, minute at least one “we considered X and held, because Y.” Around 90% of your decisions this season are decisions to hold, the trade log captures none of them, and they feed a full fifth of the rubric.
