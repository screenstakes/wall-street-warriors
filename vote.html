The vote

Ten names, five sectors, one ballot. The numbers re-checked against the filings, and what each name actually adds on top of the VOO we are about to buy.

Open the ballot

Read this before you vote

These ten are not competing to pay Laura's $50,000 a year. The Treasury ladder does that, and it is already covered — our own math says the portfolio could lose money six years running and still make every payment.

They are competing for the facility money: whatever is left in 2033 to help build the place. That is why "it pays a dividend" is not an argument here. Six of the ten pitches lean on dividends, and dividends are Pile A's job.

And the clock is 2031, not 2033. Laura has to quote co-sponsors a credible dollar range two years before she spends anything, and the only way to quote a tight one is to already be de-risked. This money has about four years to work, not seven.

Every name that gets 3 of 5 gets bought at $6,000. You can approve both in a sector, one, or neither — there is no quota and Wharton has no sector rule. A slot nobody approves stays in cash and funds the next round of picks.

There is a second vote on the ballot and it matters more than these ten. We still own nothing. VOO, VEA, both Treasuries and BIL — 79% of the portfolio — have never been voted on, and each one needs 4 of 5 under our own rule. That is one question at the front of the form.

Everything here was re-checked against the companies' SEC filings on October 2nd. Where a number differs from the sheet Coach P built, the sheet is not wrong — it faithfully recorded what we each submitted. We are the ones who submitted it.

What we fixed in the sheet

13 corrections

Coach P flagged six problems with the sheet on Thursday. All six are real. Checking them turned up five more. Nothing here is anyone's fault — every one of these numbers is what a stock screener hands you, and the screeners are wrong in ways you only see in the filings.

Alphabet 54.77%
This is the one that matters, and it is mine. It is genuinely the reported net margin — but Alphabet's last year of earnings includes $105.8B of mostly unrealised gains on shares it holds in other companies. Strip those and the margin is about 31%. Which means the 17.6× P/E I posted is really about 30×. I told you Alphabet was half price. It is not. The giveaway: its forward P/E (24.5×) is HIGHER than its trailing P/E, which never happens to a growing company. And I benchmarked it against the wrong sector on top of that — see below.
Amazon 17.4%
Same problem, different gain. Last quarter carried $53.4B of non-operating income, most of it the mark on its Anthropic stake. Clean net margin is about 12%, which lines up with its 12.1% operating margin. Same tell — trailing P/E below forward P/E.
GE Vernova 5.8%
Dylan's number is right and the screeners are wrong. Most sites show GEV at a 23% net margin, which is a $2.9B tax release plus $4.5B of one-off deal gains. The proof it is fake: GEV's yearly EBITDA ($3.9B) is <i>smaller</i> than its net income ($9.5B), which is arithmetically impossible from actually running a business.
Interactive Brokers 77%
Caleb labelled it honestly as pretax, but the sheet files it under "Profit Margin" next to J&J's 21.5%, and they are not the same thing. IBKR's public company owns only 26.5% of the operating business. Of $2,509M of profit, $1,930M goes to the other owners. Margin that actually reaches IBKR shareholders: 16.5% — less than J&J, not 3.5× more.
The two brokers' debt
HOOD at 2.4× and IBKR at 2.1× are screener errors: they count securities loaned — customer shares, not borrowing — as debt. Real corporate debt to equity is 0.23× for HOOD and 0.00× for IBKR, which holds $13M of borrowings against $5.9B of equity. Never take a debt ratio off a screener for a broker.
Booking "N/M"
Coach P was right that it is not meaningful — equity really is negative, minus $10.8B. But that is $62.1B of share buybacks, not losses. Net debt to EBITDA is 0.3×. Booking is one of the least indebted companies on this list, and the sheet makes it look like the most.
Growth rates
Coach P flagged that the periods were inconsistent, and they were. Five of us quoted a single quarter's growth next to a full year's revenue, which flatters whoever had a good quarter. On one basis — trailing year vs the year before — Alphabet is +20.1% not +24.2%, NVIDIA is +83.4% not +105.9%, GE Vernova is +13.0% not +22%, and Eaton is +15.5% not +21%.
P/E benchmarks
Coach P flagged these too, and the fix is bigger than a relabel. Alphabet is not a Technology company. GICS moved it to Communication Services in 2018. I compared it to "Technology ~30×" and it looked like a steal. Communication Services actually trades at 17.2× — so even on its flattering reported earnings Alphabet is exactly in line with its sector, not half price. Amazon is Consumer Discretionary for the same reason, not tech.
Where each one really sits
Measured against the right sector, four of the ten are clearly rich and three are clearly cheap, and that holds whichever data source you use. Rich: Robinhood at roughly 2.7× its industry, Eaton at 1.6×, Interactive Brokers at 1.9×. Cheap: Booking, Medtronic, and NVIDIA, which is actually below its own sector. J&J and GE Vernova land in line. Two caveats worth saying out loud: sector multiples vary by about five turns between data providers depending on whether they strip one-off charges, so I used one source throughout rather than mixing; and NVIDIA is 15% of the tech sector ETF while Amazon is 23% of the consumer one, so for those two "cheap versus its sector" is partly comparing them to themselves.
One more that failed the same test
Running the forward-versus-trailing check across all ten turned up a third name I had missed: GE Vernova trades at 28× trailing and 47× forward. Same signature as Alphabet and Amazon, same cause — the one-off tax release and deal gains in its reported earnings. It does not change Dylan's 5.8% margin, which was right; it means GEV's P/E is the one number of his that flatters it. On next year's earnings it is the most expensive industrial on the list, not the cheaper of the two.
J&J's 64 years
Coach P asked us to check the date. It holds. J&J announced its 64th consecutive annual increase on April 14, 2026, lifting the quarterly dividend from $1.30 to $1.34. Joe's number was right.
NVIDIA 63.66%
Also right, and worth saying because the rest of this list is corrections. That is a real net margin — its operating margin is actually higher, at 65.2%. The only fix is the P/E, 29.6× rather than 28.5×, because the stock moved.
Booking's price
Not an error, a trap for later: Booking did a 25-for-1 split on April 2. Any screenshot or note older than that shows a price 25× too high. $159 today was $3,976 before the split.

Where every number came from

Every figure above was pulled on October 2–3, 2026 and checked against the company's own filing, not a screener, wherever the two disagreed.

  • Revenue, net income, debt and equity: each company's most recent 10-Q or quarterly 8-K on sec.gov. Those filings are the reason eleven numbers on the sheet changed.
  • Prices and market caps: the close on Thursday October 2, 2026.
  • Weights in VOO: the iShares S&P 500 holdings file dated October 1, 2026, cross-checked against a second source dated October 2.
  • J&J's 64-year dividend streak: J&J's own press release of April 14, 2026.
  • Our own numbers — the $421,767 reserve, the −1.14% break-even, the 2031 de-risking date — come from the case PDFs and our team's working file.
