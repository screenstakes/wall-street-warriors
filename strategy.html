Pile A · why this works

We buy bonds that expire.

A Treasury note pays its face value on a known date. A bond fund never matures. That is what turns “probably enough” into a date on a calendar.

Unmatched, the 2033 lump sum we would need is anywhere from $402,560 to $500,000 depending on rates. Matching kills that $97,440 of guesswork.

Pile C · the part that was vague

Cash moves on three triggers. Nothing else.

Why 15%, and when it changes: three-month bills pay 4.2%, so holding cash costs us almost nothing today. If bills fall below 3%, Pile C drops to 5%. Tied to the rate, not to habit — at 2021's 0.05% this would have been indefensible.

Answering “a high degree of certainty”

We put a number on it.

97%

Two tests that must both pass: every payment matched by a bond maturing in time, and at least 97 of 100 simulated futures funding all ten.

Our plan currently measures 97.4%. The case makes us define certainty ourselves, so we define it as a rule we can be held to.

The three questions everyone asks

Already answered.

Should we add real estate?
No. REITs move almost exactly with the stock market — beta 0.98 — and fell harder than stocks in 2008, 2020 and 2022. It looks like diversification and is not.
Should Pile A hold corporate bonds?
We are not allowed to. Wharton permits government bonds and ETFs only. The single route is a corporate bond fund, and a fund never matures — which is the entire point of Pile A.
Is 15% in cash too much?
Not while bills pay 4.2%. Six-year cost is small, and it means we never sell Laura's bond money early. Below 3% it shrinks to 5%.
