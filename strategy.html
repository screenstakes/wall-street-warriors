The thing nobody had worked out

She can already afford it.

$282,682

The ten payments add up to $500,000, but you do not need $500,000 today — money set aside now earns interest until each one is due. At today’s 10-year Treasury of 5.18% they cost $282,682.

Not an inflation adjustment — every payment stays exactly $50,000, as the case requires. This is the same maths behind our $421,767 reserve figure, just measured from today instead of from 2033. Against about $420,000 of contributions, she is roughly 150% funded before we buy anything.

What we choose

Which bonds, and how fast we buy them. The list is fixed — 41 US Treasuries, and we cannot add to it.

Why the hole exists

Wharton’s list is nearly all 30-year bonds, and the Treasury stopped issuing those between 2001 and 2006 — so none mature 2032 to 2035. Notes maturing in those years do exist in the real market; they are simply not on the list we are allowed to trade.

Are they required to pay?
Yes. A bond is a legal debt. The US government has never missed a Treasury payment — which is why finance calls it the risk-free rate and measures everything else against it.
So it is 100% certain?
Nothing is literally 100%. The real risk is not refusal, it is a political fight over the debt ceiling delaying a payment. The US was downgraded in 2011 and again in 2023 — and still has not missed one.
But interest rates change. Does that break it?
No — the $50,000 is locked the day we buy. We agree the price now for a fixed amount on a fixed date, and that promise does not move afterwards. Rates change what a bond would sell for, and what the next one costs us — not what the ones we already own pay out.
Then where is the actual risk?
Selling early. Hold it to the date and you get the full $50,000. Sell before, and you get whatever the market pays that day, which can be less. So we never sell Pile A early — and that is what Pile C is for.

What “a high degree of certainty” means

Why it is 97 and not 100.

The case says the ten payments must be funded “with a high degree of certainty” and never says what that means. So we have to decide, and say it out loud.

The three questions everyone asks

Already answered.

Should we add real estate?
No. REITs move almost exactly with the stock market — beta 0.98 — and fell harder than stocks in 2008, 2020 and 2022. It looks like diversification and is not.
Should Pile A hold corporate bonds?
We are not allowed to. Wharton permits government bonds and ETFs only. The single route is a corporate bond fund, and a fund never matures — which is the entire point of Pile A.
Is 15% in cash too much?
Not while bills pay 4.2%. Six-year cost is small, and it means we never sell Laura's bond money early. Below 3% it shrinks to 5%.
