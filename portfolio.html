What is BIL, and is the reserve the right size?

It is not a company, and the letters are not an abbreviation.

BIL is just the ticker — picked to sound like “bill”. The fund is the SPDR Bloomberg 1–3 Month T-Bill ETF, run by State Street, and it holds US Treasury bills: loans to the US government repaid in one to three months.

The price barely moves.

It sits near $91 and pays interest monthly. That is the point: it is not trying to grow, it is trying not to lose. Think of a savings account you can buy and sell like a stock.

So it is where money waits.

Every dollar in BIL is a dollar not growing in Pile B. That is the trade, and it is worth making only if we can say what the waiting buys us.

BIL is 13.72% of the account — one order of 450 shares, $41,157 — and nothing else is hiding inside it. The $30,000 of company money that sat in this line until Tuesday went into the nine names, so this order is the Pile C reserve and only that.

Why BIL shrank. It was planned at 492 shares, 15% of the account, and keyed at 450 because the two Treasuries came in $3,097 over the estimate, which would have left only a 2.2% cushion at Thursday's open against 4.4% now. Cushion here is the cash left measured against the growth pile — how big a gap up at the open it can absorb before an order has to be unwound.

Where Pile C stands

It answers none of Laura's three case questions.

Pile A funds her ten payments. Pile B grows the 2033 building money. Pile C does neither — it is ours, not hers, and the case never asked for it.

The trigger exists, it was written Oct 5, and the trim did not change it.

Pile C gets spent on exactly three things: an approved name at a buy price written down in advance; VOO 10% below our cost, at most twice a season; or a 2037–42 Treasury rung yielding 5.5% or more. It never drops below a $6,000 floor. A reserve with a stated condition is a decision, and that is what criterion 4 grades.

The live question is its size. Coach P, Oct 6: shrink it to 10%.

Its own largest trigger — one Treasury rung at the 5.5% bar — is about $30,000, which is 10% of the account. A reserve bigger than the biggest thing it can buy is money not working. The freed money would go to VEA, which overlaps none of the nine and is where we are most underweight.
