What is BIL, and why so much of it?

It is not a company, and the letters are not an abbreviation.

BIL is just the ticker — picked to sound like “bill”. The fund is the SPDR Bloomberg 1–3 Month T-Bill ETF, run by State Street, and it holds US Treasury bills: loans to the US government repaid in one to three months.

The price barely moves.

It sits near $91 and pays interest monthly. That is the point: it is not trying to grow, it is trying not to lose. Think of a savings account you can buy and sell like a stock.

So it is where money waits.

Every dollar in BIL is a dollar not growing in Pile B. That is the trade, and it is worth making only if we can say what the waiting buys us.

BIL is 24.74% of the account but only 14.74% of the strategy. The account shows one line of 810 shares; the plan treats $30,000 of it as Pile B money waiting for names, and only $44,212 as the Pile C reserve.

The 24.74% is doing two different jobs. Only one of them is a decision.

The actual flex pile

This is the only part that is a choice. It exists so that when we find something worth buying, we do not have to sell a good position to afford it.

Why Pile C is the weakest thing in the portfolio

It answers none of Laura's three case questions.

Pile A funds her ten payments. Pile B grows the 2033 building money. Pile C does neither — it is ours, not hers, and the case never asked for it.

It has no declared size, owner or trigger.

The 15% is an assertion on the deck, not a decided policy. It came from Coach P's ask on Sep 20 and nobody has written down what it is for.

Without a trigger it reads as indecision.

Criterion 4 grades our decision process. A reserve with a stated condition is a decision; a reserve with no condition is money we could not place.

The decision to make this week

The IPS is due Nov 6: 550 words, no graphics, no citations. “25% in T-bills” with no stated purpose is the line a judge circles. So either give Pile C a written job — one sentence saying what has to be true for it to get spent — or shrink it and move the money to Pile B.

Write the trigger. Keep the 15%.Shrink Pile C. Move it to Pile B.
The flex pile is not free. Holding $45,000 in bills rather than growth, over the 7 years until Laura’s 2033 building money is due, is about $17,905 — $59,217 at 4% a year against $77,122 at 8%. Those rates are an illustration, not a forecast. An option that costs $17,905 has to buy something we can name, and Pile C still has no written trigger.
